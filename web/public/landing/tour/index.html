Chapter by chapter

  1. 1 Chapter I The Cyclone👻🕯️🌑

    Dorothy, an orphan, lives with her stern Aunt Em and Uncle Henry on the gray Kansas prairie, finding joy only in her dog Toto. A cyclone strikes, and while Aunt Em hides in the cellar, the house is carried into the sky with Dorothy and Toto inside. After hours of wind, Dorothy calms down and falls asleep.

    • Scariness & peril (moderate): A cyclone lifts the house with Dorothy inside; dark, howling wind, Toto falls through trap door.
    • Death & grief (mild): Dorothy is an orphan; she briefly fears being dashed to pieces when the house lands.
    • Mature themes (mild): Bleak, joyless farm life; Aunt Em and Uncle Henry worn down and never smile.
  2. 2 Chapter II The Council with the Munchkins⚔️👻🕯️🌑

    Dorothy's house lands in the beautiful land of the Munchkins, killing the Wicked Witch of the East and freeing the Munchkins. The good Witch of the North gives Dorothy the silver shoes and tells her to follow the yellow brick road to the City of Emeralds to ask Oz for help getting home. She kisses Dorothy's forehead for protection and vanishes.

    • Violence & gore (mild): Dorothy's house lands on a witch; only her feet are seen sticking out.
    • Scariness & peril (mild): Dorothy is frightened by the witch's feet and warned of deadly deserts and a wicked witch.
    • Death & grief (moderate): The Wicked Witch of the East is killed by the house and dries up in the sun.
    • Mature themes (mild): The Munchkins were held in slavery by the witch; Dorothy cries, feeling lonely and stranded.
  3. 3 Chapter III How Dorothy Saved the Scarecrow👻🕯️🌑

    Dorothy puts on the Witch of the East's silver shoes and sets off with Toto along the yellow brick road to the Emerald City. She spends a night feasting with the rich Munchkin Boq, who thinks she is a friendly witch. The next day she frees a talking Scarecrow from his pole, and he joins her hoping Oz will give him brains.

    • Scariness & peril (mild): Boq warns of rough and dangerous places ahead; Scarecrow admits he fears a lighted match.
    • Death & grief (mild): Munchkins mention Dorothy destroying the Wicked Witch; her silver shoes belonged to the dead witch.
    • Mature themes (mild): Munchkins celebrate freedom from the Witch's bondage; Scarecrow is sad about having no brains.
  4. 4 Chapter IV The Road Through the Forest👻😠

    Dorothy, Toto and the Scarecrow travel along the increasingly rough yellow brick road, and the Scarecrow tells how he was made and how a crow taught him the value of brains. Dorothy explains there is no place like home. At nightfall they enter a dark forest and rest in an empty cottage.

    • Scariness & peril (mild): The travelers enter a dark, lonely forest at nightfall and stumble along in darkness.
    • Meanness & bullying (mild): A crow mocks the Scarecrow as clumsy and straw-stuffed; Dorothy says he has no brains.
  5. 5 Chapter V The Rescue of the Tin Woodman⚔️👻🕯️💌🌑😠

    Dorothy and the Scarecrow find a rusted Tin Woodman in the forest and oil his joints to free him. He joins them to ask Oz for a heart and tells how the Wicked Witch's enchanted axe cut him apart until he was entirely tin and could no longer love. Dorothy worries as her bread runs low.

    • Violence & gore (moderate): An enchanted axe cuts off the woodman's legs, arms, head, and splits his body; told without blood.
    • Scariness & peril (mild): Dorothy hears groans in the forest; the woodman was stuck rusted for a year.
    • Death & grief (mild): The woodman mentions his father and mother dying; he thought losing his head was the end.
    • Romance & sexuality (mild): The woodman loved a Munchkin girl and planned to marry her.
    • Mature themes (mild): The woodman loses his ability to love; Dorothy worries about running out of food.
    • Meanness & bullying (mild): A lazy old woman and the Wicked Witch scheme cruelly to stop the marriage.
  6. 6 Chapter VI The Cowardly Lion⚔️👻🕯️😠

    While walking through a dark forest, the group is attacked by a Lion, and Dorothy slaps him when he tries to bite Toto. The Lion admits he is a coward and joins them to ask Oz for courage. Later the Tin Woodman cries over a beetle he stepped on, rusts his jaw, and the Scarecrow oils him.

    • Violence & gore (mild): The Lion knocks over the Scarecrow and claws the Tin Woodman; Dorothy slaps the Lion's nose.
    • Scariness & peril (mild): Growls in the dark forest; the Lion roars and nearly bites Toto.
    • Death & grief (mild): The Tin Woodman accidentally steps on a beetle and cries about it.
    • Meanness & bullying (mild): Dorothy angrily calls the Lion a big coward, and he hangs his head in shame.
  7. 7 Chapter VII The Journey to the Great Oz⚔️👻🕯️

    The travelers camp in the forest, then cross a deep ditch on the Lion's back. At a wider gulf they fell a tree as a bridge, and when the fearsome Kalidahs follow, the Woodman chops the tree so the beasts fall. They reach a river and begin building a raft, sleeping before it is finished.

    • Violence & gore (moderate): The Lion offers to kill a deer; two Kalidahs fall into a gulf and are dashed on rocks.
    • Scariness & peril (moderate): Monstrous bear-tiger Kalidahs chase the group; the Lion fears they will be torn to pieces.
    • Death & grief (mild): The two attacking Kalidahs die in the fall; briefly described without gore.
  8. 8 Chapter VIII The Deadly Poppy Field👻🕯️🍺

    The travelers cross the river on a raft, but the Scarecrow is left stuck on his pole until a kind Stork rescues him. They then wander into a huge field of poppies whose scent puts Dorothy, Toto and the Lion to sleep. The Scarecrow and Tin Woodman carry Dorothy and Toto to safety but must leave the Lion behind in the poppies.

    • Scariness & peril (moderate): The Scarecrow is stranded in a river, and the poppies' scent threatens endless sleep.
    • Death & grief (mild): The Lion warns Dorothy will die; friends sadly leave the sleeping Lion, believing he'll sleep forever.
    • Substances (mild): Poppy scent acts like a poison, putting Dorothy, Toto and the Lion to sleep.
  9. 9 Chapter IX The Queen of the Field Mice⚔️👻🕯️

    The Tin Woodman kills a Wildcat chasing the Queen of the Field Mice, who pledges her subjects' help. The Woodman builds a cart, and thousands of mice pull the sleeping Cowardly Lion out of the poisonous poppy field. Dorothy wakes, meets the Queen, and the group waits for the Lion to wake.

    • Violence & gore (moderate): The Tin Woodman cuts off a Wildcat's head with his axe to save a mouse.
    • Scariness & peril (mild): A wildcat with ugly teeth and glowing red eyes chases a mouse; the Lion is in peril among poppies.
    • Death & grief (mild): The Wildcat is killed; the Lion is rescued from possible death in the poppy field.
  10. 10 Chapter X The Guardian of the Gate👻🕯️

    The Lion wakes from the poppy sleep, and the group returns to the yellow brick road, spending the night with a kind farm family who describe the mysterious Oz. The next day they reach the Emerald City, where the Guardian of the Gates locks green spectacles on each of them before leading them inside.

    • Scariness & peril (mild): The Guardian warns that Oz might grow angry and destroy visitors who come on foolish errands.
    • Death & grief (mild): The Lion reflects that the poppies nearly killed him before the mice saved him.
  11. 11 Chapter XI The Wonderful City of Oz⚔️👻🕯️🌑😠

    Dorothy and her friends enter the dazzling Emerald City and are lodged in the Palace. Each meets Oz alone, who appears in a different form—a Head, a Lady, a Beast, a Ball of Fire—and demands they kill the Wicked Witch of the West before he grants their wishes. Saddened, they agree to set out for the land of the Winkies.

    • Violence & gore (mild): Ball of Fire singes the Lion's whiskers; Lion boasts of frightening or rolling Oz's head.
    • Scariness & peril (moderate): Oz appears as a giant bodiless Head, a five-eyed monstrous Beast, and a fierce Ball of Fire.
    • Death & grief (mild): Dorothy recalls her house killing the Witch of the East; Oz demands the Western Witch be killed.
    • Mature themes (mild): A child is told to kill someone to go home; she weeps, saying she doesn't want to kill anyone.
    • Meanness & bullying (mild): Oz coldly refuses help without payment and dismisses Dorothy while she cries.
  12. 12 Chapter XII The Search for the Wicked Witch⚔️👻🕯️🌑😠

    Dorothy and her friends head west to find the Wicked Witch, who sends wolves, crows, bees, and slaves against them, all defeated. The Witch then uses the Winged Monkeys, who wreck the Woodman and Scarecrow, capture the Lion, and bring Dorothy to the castle as a servant. When the Witch steals a Silver Shoe, Dorothy angrily throws water on her and the Witch melts away, freeing Dorothy and the Lion.

    • Violence & gore (moderate): Tin Woodman beheads forty wolves; Scarecrow twists necks of forty crows; Witch beats slaves with a strap.
    • Scariness & peril (moderate): Witch sends wolves, crows, bees, and Winged Monkeys to destroy the travelers; Dorothy is captured.
    • Death & grief (moderate): Many animals killed; the Witch melts away after Dorothy splashes her with water.
    • Mature themes (moderate): Witch enslaves the Winkies and Dorothy; Lion is starved; Dorothy cries for hours in despair.
    • Meanness & bullying (moderate): Witch threatens Dorothy, hits Toto, tricks Dorothy into falling and steals her shoe, laughing at her.
  13. 13 Chapter XIII The Rescue⚔️🕯️🌑😠

    Dorothy frees the Lion and the enslaved Winkies, who celebrate. With the Winkies' help they repair the Tin Woodman and restuff the Scarecrow. Given gifts, the friends set off for the Emerald City, Dorothy unknowingly wearing the Golden Cap.

    • Violence & gore (mild): The Tin Woodman is found battered and dented; the Scarecrow's clothes were thrown into a tree.
    • Death & grief (mild): The Witch's melting is mentioned briefly as past news.
    • Mature themes (mild): The Winkies were enslaved by the Witch for years; Dorothy frees them.
    • Meanness & bullying (mild): The Witch is said to have treated the Winkies with great cruelty.
  14. 14 Chapter XIV The Winged Monkeys⚔️👻💌🌑😠

    Lost in the flower fields, Dorothy summons the Queen of the Mice, who tells her to use the Golden Cap's charm. The Winged Monkeys carry the group to the Emerald City while their King tells how Gayelette bound his people to the Cap after a prank on Quelala.

    • Violence & gore (mild): In a story, monkeys drop Quelala in a river; a punishment of drowning is threatened but spared.
    • Scariness & peril (mild): The travelers get lost and discouraged; the Scarecrow and Woodman briefly fear the monkeys.
    • Romance & sexuality (mild): Princess Gayelette loves Quelala and marries him; brief mention of wanting someone to mate with.
    • Mature themes (mild): Monkeys describe being enslaved by the Cap and forced to enslave the Winkies.
    • Meanness & bullying (mild): Monkeys play mean pranks, tease mice, and dunk Quelala in the river as a joke.
  15. 15 Chapter XV The Discovery of Oz, the Terrible⚔️👻🕯️🌑😠

    The travelers return to the Emerald City and, after days of waiting, confront Oz, who turns out to be an ordinary old man hiding behind a screen. He explains his tricks and how a balloon brought him from Omaha. He promises the Scarecrow, Lion and Tin Woodman their gifts tomorrow and asks time to find a way to send Dorothy home.

    • Violence & gore (mild): The Tin Woodman raises his axe and rushes at the little man, who begs not to be struck.
    • Scariness & peril (mild): The empty Throne Room and booming invisible Voice feel dreadful; the Lion roars fiercely.
    • Death & grief (mild): The Witch's melting is mentioned and called good news; the East Witch's death recalled.
    • Mature themes (mild): Oz admits deceiving his people for years and making promises he couldn't keep.
    • Meanness & bullying (mild): The friends call Oz a humbug and a bad man; Oz manipulated them to fight the Witch.
  16. 16 Chapter XVI The Magic Art of the Great Humbug🍺🌑

    The Wizard gives the Scarecrow a head of bran mixed with pins and needles, cuts a hole in the Tin Woodman's chest to insert a silk heart, and has the Lion drink a liquid he calls courage. All three leave happy, while Oz privately admits he is a humbug and doesn't know how to send Dorothy home.

    • Substances (mild): The Lion drinks an unnamed liquid from a green bottle that Oz calls courage.
    • Mature themes (mild): Oz knowingly deceives his friends with fake gifts, calling himself a humbug.
  17. 17 Chapter XVII How the Balloon Was Launched👻🕯️🌑

    Oz decides to take Dorothy back to Kansas in a hot-air balloon, and they sew it from green silk. At the launch, Dorothy chases Toto into the crowd, the ropes snap, and Oz floats away without her, leaving the Scarecrow to rule. The people mourn the Wizard's loss.

    • Scariness & peril (mild): Oz mentions the balloon could fall in the desert and they'd be lost; Dorothy is left behind.
    • Death & grief (mild): The people grieve the Wizard's departure for many days; no one dies.
    • Mature themes (mild): Oz admits to deceiving his people as a humbug; Dorothy is sad and homesick.
  18. 18 Chapter XVIII Away to the South👻

    After Oz leaves in the balloon, Dorothy is sad, and the Scarecrow becomes ruler of the Emerald City. The Winged Monkeys cannot carry her to Kansas, so the green-whiskered soldier suggests asking Glinda, the Good Witch of the South. Her three friends decide to travel south with her.

    • Scariness & peril (mild): The soldier warns the southern road has wild beasts and unfriendly strange men.
  19. 19 Chapter XIX Attacked by the Fighting Trees⚔️👻

    Dorothy and her friends leave the Emerald City, saying farewell to the Guardian of the Gate, and head south toward the Land of the South. At a forest, the front row of trees seizes and throws them back until the Tin Woodman chops the branches and frees Toto. Beyond the wood they find a high wall of white china, and the Woodman plans to build a ladder.

    • Violence & gore (mild): Trees fling the Scarecrow; the Tin Woodman chops branches, and a tree shakes as if in pain.
    • Scariness & peril (mild): Fighting trees grab the travelers and shake Toto until he howls, briefly.
  20. 20 Chapter XX The Dainty China Country⚔️😠

    The travelers climb the wall on a ladder the Tin Woodman builds and find a tiny country where everything is made of china. They accidentally break a cow's leg and later a church, and Dorothy meets a china Princess who explains they would stiffen if taken away. They cross carefully and climb the far wall.

    • Violence & gore (mild): A china cow kicks and breaks her leg; the milkmaid's elbow is nicked; the Lion smashes a china church.
    • Meanness & bullying (mild): The milkmaid scolds the travelers angrily; the clown teases the Princess with a cheeky rhyme.
  21. 21 Chapter XXI The Lion Becomes the King of Beasts⚔️👻🕯️

    The travelers cross marshy land into an old forest where the Lion feels at home. Hundreds of beasts beg him to defeat a giant spider that has been eating them. The Lion kills the sleeping spider and is made King of Beasts, promising to return after Dorothy leaves for Kansas.

    • Violence & gore (moderate): The Lion knocks the sleeping spider's head off with one paw blow; legs wiggle until still.
    • Scariness & peril (moderate): A giant spider monster with foot-long teeth eats forest animals; Dorothy is briefly afraid.
    • Death & grief (moderate): The spider has eaten many animals, including all other lions; the Lion kills the spider.
  22. 22 Chapter XXII The Country of the Quadlings⚔️👻😠

    The travelers are blocked by the armless Hammer-Heads, who knock the Scarecrow and Lion down a rocky hill. Dorothy uses her last summons of the Winged Monkeys to fly over the hill into the red country of the Quadlings. After a meal at a farmhouse, they reach Glinda's castle and are admitted.

    • Violence & gore (mild): Hammer-Heads knock the Scarecrow and Lion down the hill; the Lion is bruised and sore.
    • Scariness & peril (mild): Strange armless men with shooting heads block the travelers' path.
    • Meanness & bullying (mild): The Hammer-Heads laugh harshly at the Scarecrow's tumble.
  23. 23 Chapter XXIII Glinda The Good Witch Grants Dorothy’s Wish🕯️

    Dorothy and her friends meet Glinda, who arranges for the Winged Monkeys to carry the Scarecrow, Tin Woodman, and Lion to their new kingdoms. Glinda reveals that the Silver Shoes can take Dorothy home. After tearful goodbyes, Dorothy clicks her heels and lands safely in Kansas, losing the shoes along the way.

    • Death & grief (mild): Dorothy worries Aunt Em will go into mourning; the Wicked Witch's death is mentioned in passing.
  24. 24 Chapter XXIV Home Again

    Aunt Em is watering the cabbages when she sees Dorothy running toward her. She hugs and kisses her, and Dorothy explains she has come from the Land of Oz with Toto and is glad to be home.

Characters

Dorothyheroine, little girl

Looks: Well-grown child with a merry laugh; faded blue-and-white checked gingham dress, pink sunbonnet, apron, silver shoes; mark of the Good Witch's kiss on her forehead. In the Emerald City wears a green satin gown that later turns white.

A Kansas orphan swept to Oz by a cyclone who journeys to find a way home.

TotoDorothy's dog

Looks: Little black dog with long silky hair, small twinkling black eyes, funny wee nose; later wears a ribbon and bell, then a golden collar.

Dorothy's beloved companion and her only joy in gray Kansas.

Scarecrowcompanion seeking brains

Looks: Straw-stuffed sack head with painted face and blue eyes (one bigger), old pointed blue hat, faded blue suit, blue-topped boots; later a bulging head with pins and needles and a gold-headed walking stick.

Freed from a cornfield pole, he proves clever throughout and ends as ruler of the Emerald City.

Tin Woodmancompanion seeking a heart

Looks: Man made entirely of shining tin with jointed limbs and jaw hinges; carries an axe (later gold-handled) and a jeweled oil-can; patched after repairs, square patch on left breast.

A tender-hearted woodman who rusts when he cries and wishes to love again.

Cowardly Lioncompanion seeking courage

Looks: As big as a small horse, shaggy mane, sharp claws, long tail, whiskers; later a golden collar.

A frightened lion who acts bravely again and again and becomes King of Beasts.

Ozthe Wizard

Looks: Appears as a huge bald Head, a winged green-haired Lady, a five-eyed Beast, and a Ball of Fire; truly a little old man with a bald head and wrinkled face.

A balloonist from Omaha who rules by tricks and admits he is a humbug.

Wicked Witch of the Westvillain

Ruler of the Winkies who enslaves Dorothy and melts when doused with water.

Gives Dorothy the silver shoes and a protective kiss and points her to Oz.

GlindaGood Witch of the South

Places

  • Kansas prairie: Flat, treeless gray land baked by the sun, cracked earth and gray burned grass; small one-room gray house with a trap door to a cyclone cellar.
  • Land of the Munchkins: Green grass, fruit trees, bright flowers, colorful birds, sparkling brook, round blue houses with domed roofs.
  • Yellow brick road: Road paved with yellow bricks, lined with blue fences; rough and broken in the forest, smooth near the city.
  • The great forest: Big close trees with branches meeting overhead, dark beneath, dried leaves on the road.
  • Poppy field: A great meadow carpeted with brilliant scarlet poppies whose scent causes deep sleep.
  • Emerald City: High bright green wall with emerald-studded gate; green marble houses and streets studded with emeralds, green glow in the sky.
  • Throne Room: Big round domed room covered in emeralds, green marble throne, a screen in the corner hiding Oz.
  • Country of the Winkies: Rough, hilly yellow land without trees or farms; the Witch's yellow castle with a yard and high iron fence.
  • Dainty China Country: Smooth shining white floor like a platter, tiny brightly painted china houses, barns, fences and animals, ringed by a white china wall.
  • Forest of the beasts: Huge old trees, soft dried leaves, rich green moss, and a clearing where animals gather.
  • Country of the Quadlings: Rich grain fields, paved roads, brooks with bridges, everything painted bright red.
  • Glinda's Castle: Very beautiful castle guarded by girls in red uniforms with gold braid; a big room with a throne of rubies.

Plot threads

  • Dorothy's constant goal is to return home to Aunt Em and Uncle Henry in Kansas.
  • The silver shoes taken from the Witch of the East hold the power to send Dorothy home, unknown until Glinda reveals it.
  • The Scarecrow, Tin Woodman, and Lion each seek a gift from Oz yet already show the qualities they think they lack.
  • Oz demands the defeat of the Wicked Witch of the West before granting any wishes.
  • The Golden Cap commands the Winged Monkeys three times, and Dorothy uses all three summons.
  • Oz is revealed as an ordinary man from Omaha and leaves in a balloon without Dorothy after she chases Toto.
  • Each friend ends with a kingdom: Scarecrow in the Emerald City, Tin Woodman with the Winkies, Lion in the forest.

Key themes

  • There is no place like home
  • Friendship and loyalty
  • Courage, brains and heart lie within
  • Appearances can deceive
  • Kindness to the small

The story so far… Dorothy lived on a gray Kansas farm with Aunt Em, Uncle Henry, and her little dog Toto, until a giant whirling cyclone lifted her whole house into the sky! The house landed in the bright, beautiful land of the Munchkins, right on top of the Wicked Witch of the East, and the Munchkins were set free. The kind Witch of the North gave Dorothy a pair of silver shoes and a magic kiss, and told her to follow the yellow brick road to the Emerald City, where the Great Oz might help her get home.

When Dorothy was left alone she began to feel hungry. So she went to the cupboard and cut herself some bread, which she spread with butter. She gave some to Toto, and taking a pail from the shelf she carried it down to the little brook and filled it with clear, sparkling water. Toto ran over to the trees and began to bark at the birds sitting there. Dorothy went to get him, and saw such delicious fruit hanging from the branches that she gathered some of it, finding it just what she wanted to go with her breakfast.

Then she went back to the house, and after she and Toto had each had a good drink of the cool, clear water, she set about getting ready for the journey to the City of Emeralds.

Dorothy had only one other dress, but that happened to be clean and was hanging on a peg beside her bed. It was gingham, with checks of white and blue; and although the blue was somewhat faded from many washings, it was still a pretty dress. The girl washed herself carefully, dressed herself in the clean gingham, and tied her pink sunbonnet on her head. She took a little basket and filled it with bread from the cupboard, laying a white cloth over the top. Then she looked down at her feet and noticed how old and worn her shoes were.

“They surely will never do for a long journey, Toto,” she said. And Toto looked up into her face with his little black eyes and wagged his tail to show he knew what she meant.

At that moment Dorothy saw lying on the table the silver shoes that had belonged to the Witch of the East.

“I wonder if they will fit me,” she said to Toto. “They would be just the thing to take a long walk in, for they could not wear out.”

She took off her old leather shoes and tried on the silver ones, which fitted her as well as if they had been made for her.

Finally she picked up her basket.

“Come along, Toto,” she said. “We will go to the Emerald City and ask the Great Oz how to get back to Kansas again.”

She closed the door, locked it, and put the key carefully in the pocket of her dress. And so, with Toto trotting along seriously behind her, she started on her journey.

There were several roads nearby, but it did not take her long to find the one paved with yellow bricks. Soon she was walking quickly toward the Emerald City, her silver shoes tinkling merrily on the hard, yellow road. The sun shone bright and the birds sang sweetly, and Dorothy did not feel nearly so bad as you might think a little girl would who had been suddenly whisked away from her own country and set down in the middle of a strange land.

She was surprised, as she walked along, to see how pretty the country was all around her. There were neat fences at the sides of the road, painted a dainty blue color, and beyond them were fields of grain and vegetables, plenty of them. Clearly the Munchkins were good farmers and able to grow big crops. Once in a while she would pass a house, and the people came out to look at her and bow low as she went by; for everyone knew she was the one who had gotten rid of the Wicked Witch and set them free from her cruel rule. The houses of the Munchkins were odd-looking homes, for each was round, with a big dome for a roof. All were painted blue, for in this country of the East blue was the favorite color.

Toward evening, when Dorothy was tired from her long walk and began to wonder where she should spend the night, she came to a house rather larger than the rest. On the green lawn in front of it many men and women were dancing. Five little fiddlers played as loudly as they could, and the people were laughing and singing, while a big table nearby was loaded with delicious fruits and nuts, pies and cakes, and many other good things to eat.

The people greeted Dorothy kindly, and invited her to supper and to spend the night with them; for this was the home of one of the richest Munchkins in the land, and his friends were gathered with him to celebrate being free at last from the Wicked Witch.

Dorothy ate a hearty supper and was served by the rich Munchkin himself, whose name was Boq. Then she sat upon a little sofa and watched the people dance.

When Boq saw her silver shoes he said, “You must be a great sorceress.”

“Why?” asked the girl.

“Because you wear silver shoes and have done away with the Wicked Witch. Besides, you have white in your dress, and only witches and sorceresses wear white.”

“My dress is blue and white checked,” said Dorothy, smoothing out the wrinkles in it.

“It is kind of you to wear that,” said Boq. “Blue is the color of the Munchkins, and white is the witch color. So we know you are a friendly witch.”

Dorothy did not know what to say to this, for all the people seemed to think she was a witch, and she knew very well she was only an ordinary little girl who had come by the chance of a cyclone into a strange land.

When she had grown tired of watching the dancing, Boq led her into the house, where he gave her a room with a pretty bed in it. The sheets were made of blue cloth, and Dorothy slept soundly in them till morning, with Toto curled up on the blue rug beside her.

She ate a hearty breakfast, and watched a wee Munchkin baby, who played with Toto and pulled his tail and crowed and laughed in a way that greatly amused Dorothy. Toto was a wonderful curiosity to all the people, for they had never seen a dog before.

“How far is it to the Emerald City?” the girl asked.

“I do not know,” answered Boq seriously, “for I have never been there. It is better for people to keep away from Oz, unless they have business with him. But it is a long way to the Emerald City, and it will take you many days. The country here is rich and pleasant, but you must pass through rough and dangerous places before you reach the end of your journey.”

This worried Dorothy a little, but she knew that only the Great Oz could help her get to Kansas again, so she bravely decided not to turn back.

She said good-bye to her friends, and again started along the road of yellow brick. When she had gone several miles she thought she would stop to rest, and so climbed to the top of the fence beside the road and sat down. There was a great cornfield beyond the fence, and not far away she saw a Scarecrow, placed high on a pole to keep the birds away from the ripe corn.

Dorothy leaned her chin upon her hand and gazed thoughtfully at the Scarecrow. Its head was a small sack stuffed with straw, with eyes, nose, and mouth painted on it to make a face. An old, pointed blue hat, that had belonged to some Munchkin, was perched on his head, and the rest of him was a blue suit of clothes, worn and faded, which had also been stuffed with straw. On the feet were some old boots with blue tops, such as every man wore in this country, and the figure was held up above the stalks of corn by the pole stuck up its back.

While Dorothy was looking closely into the funny, painted face of the Scarecrow, she was surprised to see one of the eyes slowly wink at her. She thought she must have been mistaken at first, for none of the scarecrows in Kansas ever wink; but soon the figure nodded its head to her in a friendly way. Then she climbed down from the fence and walked up to it, while Toto ran around the pole and barked.

“Good day,” said the Scarecrow, in a rather husky voice.

“Did you speak?” asked the girl, in wonder.

“Certainly,” answered the Scarecrow. “How do you do?”

“I’m pretty well, thank you,” replied Dorothy politely. “How do you do?”

“I’m not feeling well,” said the Scarecrow, with a smile, “for it is very dull and boring being perched up here night and day to scare away crows.”

“Can’t you get down?” asked Dorothy.

“No, for this pole is stuck up my back. If you will please take away the pole I shall be very thankful to you.”

Dorothy reached up both arms and lifted the figure off the pole, for, being stuffed with straw, it was quite light.

“Thank you very much,” said the Scarecrow, when he had been set down on the ground. “I feel like a new man.”

Dorothy was puzzled at this, for it sounded strange to hear a stuffed man speak, and to see him bow and walk along beside her.

“Who are you?” asked the Scarecrow when he had stretched himself and yawned. “And where are you going?”

“My name is Dorothy,” said the girl, “and I am going to the Emerald City, to ask the Great Oz to send me back to Kansas.”

“Where is the Emerald City?” he asked. “And who is Oz?”

“Why, don’t you know?” she answered, in surprise.

“No, indeed. I don’t know anything. You see, I am stuffed, so I have no brains at all,” he answered sadly.

“Oh,” said Dorothy, “I’m awfully sorry for you.”

“Do you think,” he asked, “if I go to the Emerald City with you, that Oz would give me some brains?”

“I cannot tell,” she answered, “but you may come with me, if you like. If Oz will not give you any brains you will be no worse off than you are now.”

“That is true,” said the Scarecrow. “You see,” he went on, in a low, secret sort of voice, “I don’t mind my legs and arms and body being stuffed, because I cannot get hurt. If anyone steps on my toes or sticks a pin into me, it doesn’t matter, for I can’t feel it. But I do not want people to call me a fool, and if my head stays stuffed with straw instead of with brains, as yours is, how am I ever to know anything?”

“I understand how you feel,” said the little girl, who was truly sorry for him. “If you will come with me I’ll ask Oz to do all he can for you.”

“Thank you,” he answered gratefully.

They walked back to the road. Dorothy helped him over the fence, and they started along the path of yellow brick for the Emerald City.

Toto did not like this new member of the party at first. He sniffed around the stuffed man as if he thought there might be a nest of rats in the straw, and he often growled in an unfriendly way at the Scarecrow.

“Don’t mind Toto,” said Dorothy to her new friend. “He never bites.”

“Oh, I’m not afraid,” replied the Scarecrow. “He can’t hurt the straw. Do let me carry that basket for you. I shall not mind it, for I can’t get tired. I’ll tell you a secret,” he went on, as he walked along. “There is only one thing in the world I am afraid of.”

“What is that?” asked Dorothy; “the Munchkin farmer who made you?”

“No,” answered the Scarecrow; “it’s a lighted match.”

When Boq saw her silver shoes he said, “You must be a great sorceress.”

“Why?” asked the girl.

“Because you wear silver shoes and have done away with the Wicked Witch. Besides, you have white in your dress, and only witches and sorceresses wear white.”

“My dress is blue and white checked,” said Dorothy, smoothing out the wrinkles in it.

“It is kind of you to wear that,” said Boq. “Blue is the color of the Munchkins, and white is the witch color. So we know you are a friendly witch.”

Dorothy did not know what to say to this, for all the people seemed to think she was a witch, and she knew very well she was only an ordinary little girl who had come by the chance of a cyclone into a strange land.

When she had grown tired of watching the dancing, Boq led her into the house, where he gave her a room with a pretty bed in it. The sheets were made of blue cloth, and Dorothy slept soundly in them till morning, with Toto curled up on the blue rug beside her.

She ate a hearty breakfast, and watched a wee Munchkin baby, who played with Toto and pulled his tail and crowed and laughed in a way that greatly amused Dorothy. Toto was a wonderful curiosity to all the people, for they had never seen a dog before.

“How far is it to the Emerald City?” the girl asked.

“I do not know,” answered Boq seriously, “for I have never been there. It is better for people to keep away from Oz, unless they have business with him. But it is a long way to the Emerald City, and it will take you many days. The country here is rich and pleasant, but you must pass through rough and dangerous places before you reach the end of your journey.”

This worried Dorothy a little, but she knew that only the Great Oz could help her get to Kansas again, so she bravely decided not to turn back.

She said good-bye to her friends, and again started along the road of yellow brick. When she had gone several miles she thought she would stop to rest, and so climbed to the top of the fence beside the road and sat down. There was a great cornfield beyond the fence, and not far away she saw a Scarecrow, placed high on a pole to keep the birds away from the ripe corn.

Dorothy leaned her chin upon her hand and gazed thoughtfully at the Scarecrow. Its head was a small sack stuffed with straw, with eyes, nose, and mouth painted on it to make a face. An old, pointed blue hat, that had belonged to some Munchkin, was perched on his head, and the rest of him was a blue suit of clothes, worn and faded, which had also been stuffed with straw. On the feet were some old boots with blue tops, such as every man wore in this country, and the figure was held up above the stalks of corn by the pole stuck up its back.

While Dorothy was looking closely into the funny, painted face of the Scarecrow, she was surprised to see one of the eyes slowly wink at her. She thought she must have been mistaken at first, for none of the scarecrows in Kansas ever wink; but soon the figure nodded its head to her in a friendly way. Then she climbed down from the fence and walked up to it, while Toto ran around the pole and barked.

“Good day,” said the Scarecrow, in a rather husky voice.

“Did you speak?” asked the girl, in wonder.

“Certainly,” answered the Scarecrow. “How do you do?”

“I’m pretty well, thank you,” replied Dorothy politely. “How do you do?”

“I’m not feeling well,” said the Scarecrow, with a smile, “for it is very dull and boring being perched up here night and day to scare away crows.”

“Can’t you get down?” asked Dorothy.

“No, for this pole is stuck up my back. If you will please take away the pole I shall be very thankful to you.”

Dorothy reached up both arms and lifted the figure off the pole, for, being stuffed with straw, it was quite light.

“Thank you very much,” said the Scarecrow, when he had been set down on the ground. “I feel like a new man.”

Dorothy was puzzled at this, for it sounded strange to hear a stuffed man speak, and to see him bow and walk along beside her.

“Who are you?” asked the Scarecrow when he had stretched himself and yawned. “And where are you going?”

“My name is Dorothy,” said the girl, “and I am going to the Emerald City, to ask the Great Oz to send me back to Kansas.”

“Where is the Emerald City?” he asked. “And who is Oz?”

“Why, don’t you know?” she answered, in surprise.

“No, indeed. I don’t know anything. You see, I am stuffed, so I have no brains at all,” he answered sadly.

“Oh,” said Dorothy, “I’m awfully sorry for you.”

“Do you think,” he asked, “if I go to the Emerald City with you, that Oz would give me some brains?”

“I cannot tell,” she answered, “but you may come with me, if you like. If Oz will not give you any brains you will be no worse off than you are now.”

“That is true,” said the Scarecrow. “You see,” he went on, in a low, secret sort of voice, “I don’t mind my legs and arms and body being stuffed, because I cannot get hurt. If anyone steps on my toes or sticks a pin into me, it doesn’t matter, for I can’t feel it. But I do not want people to call me a fool, and if my head stays stuffed with straw instead of with brains, as yours is, how am I ever to know anything?”

“I understand how you feel,” said the little girl, who was truly sorry for him. “If you will come with me I’ll ask Oz to do all he can for you.”

“Thank you,” he answered gratefully.

They walked back to the road. Dorothy helped him over the fence, and they started along the path of yellow brick for the Emerald City.

Toto did not like this new member of the party at first. He sniffed around the stuffed man as if he thought there might be a nest of rats in the straw, and he often growled in an unfriendly way at the Scarecrow.

“Don’t mind Toto,” said Dorothy to her new friend. “He never bites.”

“Oh, I’m not afraid,” replied the Scarecrow. “He can’t hurt the straw. Do let me carry that basket for you. I shall not mind it, for I can’t get tired. I’ll tell you a secret,” he went on, as he walked along. “There is only one thing in the world I am afraid of.”

“What is that?” asked Dorothy; “the Munchkin farmer who made you?”

“No,” answered the Scarecrow; “it’s a lighted match.”
